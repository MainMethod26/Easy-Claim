What is happening: one claim, six gates, and one experimental signal

EasyClaim is an insurance claims backend on Cloudflare Workers with a Flutter app on top. A customer starts a claim, an insurer's assessor moves it through a fixed lifecycle, a manager decides and pays. The cyber track made every step of that lifecycle enforce who may do what. The quantum track added one advisory signal at the Screening step, and nowhere else.

Cyber track

Make the lifecycle unbreakable

Phases 0 to 3. Every phase shipped with tests, live attack logs, a written report and a threat-to-control map. The rule was "never call planned work implemented".

Quantum track

Add a credible, honest quantum use case

Phase 4. A quantum-kernel anomaly signal compared fairly with a classical baseline. Phase 5 (post-quantum signatures) is the planned second half.

What we built: the cyber track

The backend started as a demo with a spoofable header for identity. It now has a verified identity boundary, tenant isolation for insurer staff, object-level ownership checks, a central state machine and an audit trail that cannot be edited.

Phase 0: foundation

  • Ownership check on every claim route. A claim you may not see returns the same 404 as one that does not exist, and the attempt is audited.
  • Central state machine: only legal transitions, each with the roles allowed to make it. Replay-safe.
  • Strict request schemas. Any unknown field, including stage, status, riskScore or tenantId, is rejected with 400.
  • Append-only audit table protected by database triggers.
  • Security headers, CORS allowlist, 64 KB body limit, rate limits per IP and per actor, generic error bodies.

Phase 1: identity and tenants

  • HS256 JWT verified by the app: pinned algorithm, issuer, audience, required expiry, per-role maximum lifetime. Fails closed when the secret is missing.
  • Actor is { id, role, tenantId }. Insurer staff must carry a tenant; customers never do.
  • Tenants table and tenant column on policies, claims and audit rows. A claim inherits its policy's insurer, never a client value.
  • Insurer endpoints for verify, screen, review, request-info, decide and pay, all through the state machine.
  • Stage change and its audit row are written in one database batch, so they always exist together.

Phase 3: decisions and payout

  • Decision is manager-only. Insert-only decision record with who, when, reason, amounts, payout-destination snapshot and rules version.
  • Payout is simulated: no request body accepted, amount can never exceed the claimed amount, destination must match the decision-time snapshot, one payout per claim with idempotency key.
  • Reserved columns for the Phase 2 evidence digest, the Phase 4 risk signal and the Phase 5 signature.

The regression we caught on merge day

A backend commit replaced the app entry point with an unmounted MVC layer, which silently removed authentication, role checks, tenant checks, headers, limits and the queue consumer. Typecheck failed and all 12 test files failed to load. The Phase 4 rebase restored the secured entry point and left the MVC files unmounted, and the findings are recorded as handoff item BACKEND-SEC-021. This is a good story for judges: the test suite is what caught it.

What we built: the quantum track

We did not build a quantum fraud detector. We built an experimental quantum-kernel anomaly signal for the Screening stage and compared it, fairly, against a classical baseline using the same features and preprocessing. On the synthetic data we had, the classical model was slightly ahead. We say so.

Measured result on synthetic data

Synthetic anomalies flagged at UNUSUAL or higher, per recipe. 120 synthetic normal claims as the reference. Hover a bar for the exact count.

The honest reading

  • Both models catch every late report.
  • Classical clearly wins on night-time minimal narratives.
  • Quantum is marginally ahead on serial claimants, which both mostly miss.
  • Rank correlation between the two models is 0.84. They mostly agree.
  • Quantum fit and score took 1.9 seconds on the simulator against 0.02 seconds classical.
  • Kernel checks passed: symmetric, unit diagonal, positive semidefinite, and the statevector path matched the circuit path exactly.

So: quantum did not outperform the classical baseline. That is the result, and it is exactly what the master plan told us to report if it happened.

Why one-class and not a classifier

The repository has no fraud labels. A supervised model would have needed invented labels. A one-class model learns what normal claims look like and scores how far a claim sits from them. No labels needed, no fabrication.

Features (5 qubits)

Days to report, category, prior claim count, narrative word count, submission hour. All derived from columns that already exist. No claim amount exists yet, so none was invented.

Security boundary

No API route writes a score. Reads pass the same token, role, tenant and ownership gates. Database constraints reject scores outside 0 to 1. A high score leaves the claim row byte-identical.

Simulator, not hardware

PennyLane default.qubit statevector simulator. Every stored signal says execution: simulator. A full 120-row kernel would need 23,460 circuit runs on real hardware.

What judges will want to hear

About GKHack26 specifically

GKHack26 is the Geekulcha Annual Hackathon 2026. Its published focus areas are quality education and skills, smart digital solutions for tourism, empowering township businesses through digital innovation, and platforms that accelerate open source work in Africa. We could not find a published judging rubric. So anchor the pitch on impact for ordinary South Africans, a working demo, evidence over claims, and honesty about limits. If the organisers hand out a rubric on the day, map each line of the pitch to it.

The three-minute pitch

Problem. Insurance claims in South Africa are slow, opaque and easy to abuse. A claimant cannot see where their claim is, an insurer cannot trust what arrives, and one leaked ID lets someone read another person's claim.

What EasyClaim is. A claims backend where every step of a claim's life is a controlled transition, every request proves who it is and which insurer it belongs to, and every change is written to an audit trail that cannot be edited.

What we proved. 213 automated security tests run on every change. We attacked our own server live: cross-tenant reads, forged tokens, clients setting their own claim status, replayed payouts. Every attempt is blocked and logged, and we can show the raw responses.

The quantum part. At the Screening stage we added an experimental quantum-kernel anomaly signal and compared it fairly with a classical baseline on identical features. On our synthetic data the classical model was slightly ahead. We report that honestly, because a screening signal that a human reviews is the right place to experiment, and the right way to talk about quantum today.

What is next. Evidence hashing and OCR isolation, and post-quantum signatures over decision records so a claim decision stays verifiable after quantum computers break today's signatures.

Demo, in order (about 5 minutes)

  1. Show docs/security/ATTACK_SCENARIOS.md open next to the terminal. Say: "everything you are about to see is repeatable."
  2. Customer creates a claim, fills screening, submits. Point at the audit row appearing.
  3. Assessor verifies then screens claim_demo_normal: the response carries riskSignals.interpretation: NORMAL.
  4. Assessor screens claim_demo_unusual: HIGH_ANOMALY, with the explanation text. Read it aloud: "not a fraud finding".
  5. Attack: an assessor from another insurer reads the signal, 404. A customer reads it, 403. No token, 401. A POST to the signal route, 404.
  6. Assessor moves the claim to Review by hand. Say: "the model informed, the human decided."
  7. Open quantum/results/results.md: show the recipe table where classical wins one recipe and quantum another.

Before the demo: import first, then start the server, then wait until a request returns 401. On the demo laptop the dev server takes a few minutes to answer after start.

Questions judges will ask, and answers that hold

Is quantum actually better than the classical method?
No, not on our data. Classical found 9 of 16 synthetic anomalies in its top 16, quantum found 8, and quantum was about 100 times slower on a simulator. We built the experiment so the comparison is fair, and we report the measured result. The value is a credible, correctly-bounded quantum use case inside a real security architecture, not a speed-up.
Did you run on a real quantum computer?
No. Everything ran on a local statevector simulator. Every stored signal is marked "simulator". The circuit path a hardware backend would run exists and is tested against the simulator path, and a hardware run is listed as optional future work that would be reported separately.
Where did your fraud data come from?
We have no fraud labels and refused to invent any. The experiment uses a synthetic development dataset, clearly labelled, with anomalies generated by three named recipes. That is why we chose one-class anomaly detection: it learns what normal looks like and needs no labels.
Could the model reject a claim or block a payout?
No. The signal is written by an offline pipeline into a server-only table. No API route accepts a score. The Worker reads it after the Screening transition already happened and attaches it as context. Tests prove a high-anomaly row leaves the claim row byte-identical, and moving a claim to Review still requires an assessor.
How do you know your security controls work?
213 automated tests in the Workers runtime, run on every change, plus live attack logs saved from the running server for each phase. On merge day the suite caught a commit that had removed authentication from the entry point. That is the proof it is not decoration.
What about the mobile app, is it secure?
The Flutter app is treated as an untrusted client. Every control is server-side. The threat model says so explicitly, and nothing in the API trusts a header or body field for identity, tenant or state.
Are you compliant with OWASP or NIST?
We used OWASP API Security Top 10, ASVS, NIST SSDF and FIPS 204 as reference frameworks to structure the work. We do not claim certification or compliance. Each control is mapped to the threat it addresses and the test that proves it.
What is post-quantum here, and why is it separate from quantum computing?
Phase 5 plans ML-DSA signatures over the decision bundle so a decision stays verifiable even against future quantum attackers. That is cryptography defending against quantum machines. Phase 4 uses a quantum algorithm to compute a signal. Different problems, and we keep the vocabulary separate on purpose.
What would you do with more time?
Evidence hashing and OCR isolation with prompt-injection treated as hostile data; real per-tenant data to refit the anomaly model; claim amount and evidence features; a hardware run of a small kernel block; ML-DSA signing; and the assessor-prioritisation optimisation idea from our teammate as a downstream consumer of the signal.
Why should a judge care about township businesses or ordinary people here?
A claim that cannot be read by the wrong person, cannot be silently changed, and cannot be paid twice protects the customer as much as the insurer. Faster, transparent screening with a human in the loop lowers the cost of insuring small businesses and households that insurers often treat as too risky to serve well.

Say this

  • "Experimental quantum-kernel anomaly detection compared with a classical baseline on identical features."
  • "Advisory signal. A human moves the claim."
  • "Simulator only, and every stored result says so."
  • "We report the measured result."
  • "Every control has a test and a live attack log."

Never say this

  • "Quantum detects fraud." or "Quantum proved this claim is fraudulent."
  • "Quantum is faster" or "more accurate" (it was not, here).
  • "We run on a quantum computer."
  • "We are OWASP or NIST compliant."
  • "The AI decides."
